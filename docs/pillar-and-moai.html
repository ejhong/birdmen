What about a V-neck, bent knees or an egg on the pillar?

A V-shaped line need not depict a garment; a stylised joint need not be a human knee. A bird’s knee bends forward; the visible backward bend is usually its ankle. Anatomical explanation.

Egg, celestial body and head are proposed readings of the pillar’s disc. Compare shape and position while keeping meaning open.

03 / Recover the context

The rock with a bird has a name.

The Orongo birdman boulder: British Museum Oc1920,0506.1.

The museum identifies this as British Museum Oc1920,0506.1, collected in 1914–15. Its broad creation estimate, 1750–1875, is a catalogue attribution rather than a direct date.

Routledge’s 1919 figure 112 calls the rounded object an egg. Her account of the birdman competition is an early, mediated interpretation.

The relief is visible in 1919 without the dark outlines of the circulating photograph. Those markings’ origin and date remain untraced.

Museum record · 1919 photograph and account. The museum’s 50 cm boulder width and Routledge’s 36.5 cm carving length measure different things.

How distinctive is a crouched avian figure beside a rounded object? That needs a broader, documented comparison set.

04 / Locate in time

A statue can carry more than one history.

Dating the stone figure does not automatically date the birdmen carved into its back.

The moai’s back was carved in successive episodes. The museum’s c. 1000–1200 CE statue estimate and recent settlement estimates around 1250 CE do not directly date the later birdmen. Museum record · settlement context.

05 / Explain

What would each history predict?

Select a possible history to inspect its predictions and missing evidence.

A design inherited through a very long history.

An older symbolic tradition could fragment, change meaning and survive in distant descendants. Hancock’s proposal is one possible version of a much broader inheritance hypothesis.

Evidence that would strengthen it
A rare combination of shapes and relationships recurring through independently dated intermediate contexts, accompanied by other linked practices or material evidence.
What we have here
Resembling endpoints, ambiguous meanings and a very large chronological gap. No documented transmission sequence joining these particular designs has been established in this project.
The next discriminating observation
Find and date intermediate examples before choosing which features count as a match. Their differences should form a plausible pattern of change.

A motif carried by travellers or exchange.

Contact can transmit images without wholesale migration or a single worldwide civilisation.

Evidence that would strengthen it
Overlapping dates, a plausible sequence of encounters, and linked artefacts, technologies, words or ancestry. Independent evidence should point to the same route and period.
What we have here
Evidence for Polynesian–American contact is relevant to the possibility of ocean travel. It neither dates nor traces a connection between Pillar 43 and the Rapanui carvings.
The next discriminating observation
Test a specific route and time window against the earliest local appearance of the proposed design.

Different people arrive at similar forms.

Birds, eggs, bodies and stone surfaces offer recurring subjects and constraints. Shared human experience could make some combinations likely to recur.

Evidence that would strengthen it
Well-documented local predecessors, distinct local meanings, and similar feature combinations appearing repeatedly in a broad comparison set.
What we have here
Rapa Nui has a substantial local birdman tradition; Pillar 43 sits among an Anatolian repertoire of animals and signs. How unusual this exact resemblance is has not yet been measured reliably.
The next discriminating observation
Measure specific relations—what holds, faces or sits above what—against a representative set, after validating the observers.

The presentation concentrates a partial resemblance.

Choosing two photographs, cropping a scene or emphasising an outline can make some features dominate attention.

Evidence that would strengthen it
Ratings change substantially with whole surfaces, alternate views, equal resolution, or inclusion of omitted examples.
What we have here
The old pilots were sensitive to object type and their judges disagreed. A circulating illustration differs from the photographs. These are reasons to test presentation effects, not to dismiss every resemblance.
The next discriminating observation
Randomise faithful views and balanced alternatives, then compare human and model descriptions before giving either historical context.
06 / Where this leaves us

We can sharpen the mystery. We cannot yet close it.

Some visible resemblances survive inspection. Their rarity and historical relationship remain unresolved.

We now have an identified boulder, an early photograph and a clearer carving chronology. The next tests must validate the observer and broaden the examples.

  1. Check recognition and location. The first diagnostic exposed recognition failures. The controlled follow-up improved labels but found scorpions located in the bird area. Matching a label is not enough.
  2. Build the local families. The new illustrated context study adds a nine-object register, Pillar 2 and the Rapa Nui museum’s paired birdmen. Our working explanation begins with these local traditions. Specialist review and a broader corpus remain necessary.
  3. Test resemblance fairly. Compare shapes, relationships and layout separately; include close alternatives, equal image quality and a human reference set. The replacement protocol.
  4. Ask the historical question. If a robust, unusual match survives, investigate a dated chain of transmission. If it does not, explain which apparent correspondences changed and why.
