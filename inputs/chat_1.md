Step one, today: check what your eye was actually looking at. The Göbekli Tepe/moai poster from your first screenshot is AI-generated. The big raised-arm figure on that moai's back is not what's carved on Hoa Hakananai'a. The real back, documented by the British Museum's 2014 photogrammetric study (Pitts, Miles, Pagi and Earl, Antiquaries Journal 94), has two facing birdmen at the top with a small bird between them, komari (vulva) symbols, a ring-and-girdle design at the small of the back, and dance paddles carved on the ears, all added to a much older statue during the Orongo birdman cult. Build a three-panel figure: poster, real Hoa Hakananai'a (British Museum photos and 3D model), real Pillar 43 (DAI photos and Schmidt's drawing). This takes an afternoon, and it's the difference between "my eye is lying" and "my eye reported accurately on a picture manufactured to match." I'd bet on the second.

Step two, this week: turn the pictures into a closed inventory. Your rongorongo test worked because both sides were complete catalogues. Do the same for composition:

Fixed sample. Every published relief pillar from Göbekli Tepe, not just 43. Every recorded Rapa Nui birdman surface: the Orongo petroglyph corpus (Georgia Lee's 1992 catalogue has hundreds of birdman figures) plus the handful of moai with back carvings. Then controls chosen before coding: complete bird-heavy relief or painting corpora from 15–20 unrelated traditions. Çatalhöyük vulture murals, Nevalı Çori, Egyptian stelae, Assyrian apkallu panels, Izapa stelae, Moche, Nazca textiles, Northwest Coast crest art, Norse picture stones, Shang bronzes, Aboriginal rock art. Include the Mississippian Birdman (Cahokia and Etowah copper plates, falcon-costumed humans, c. 1000–1400 CE): an independent birdman tradition with well-documented local meaning, which is exactly the kind of control that tells you what convergence looks like.
Fixed feature list, pre-registered. Not just elements but arrangement: number of registers, vertical order of classes (bird / human / hybrid / reptile / disc / container / geometric band), facing pairs and bilateral symmetry, disc present and where, bird posture (profile, spread wings), hybrid type (bird-headed human vs. bird with human limbs), position of the anthropomorph, size hierarchy, container-shaped objects and their position. That's the "how they're drawn and arranged" part, made codable.
Blind coding. Two or three vision models plus a human subset; drop any feature with Krippendorff's alpha under 0.67, before analysis.
The right null. This is where the sharpshooter problem bites hardest: the moai was picked as Pillar 43's match from all of world art. So the question isn't "how similar are these two" but "how similar is any panel to its best match in an unrelated culture." Compute compositional distance for every cross-culture pair, take each panel's nearest foreign neighbour, and see where Pillar 43↔Hoa Hakananai'a falls in that distribution. Also report Pillar 43's actual nearest neighbours; if they're other Göbekli pillars, then Çatalhöyük, then Egypt, that's informative on its own.

Pre-register the predictions so the test can lose. Mine: at least half the poster correspondences vanish against the real objects; Pillar 43's nearest neighbour outside Anatolia is not on Rapa Nui; the pair sits inside the central 90% of nearest-neighbour similarities; and every element on the moai's back is accounted for by Orongo ethnography (the tangata manu competition, the manutara egg, Makemake, komari fertility) without an external source. Your win condition: the pair in the top 5%, driven by arbitrary features (identical register order, the same handle-shaped objects across the top of both real panels, a facing-bird-and-disc configuration), not by "birds up, snakes down," which is near-universal vertical cosmology. If that happens, it's news, and I'd say so.
