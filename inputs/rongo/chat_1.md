What you're looking at is Guillaume de Hevesy's 1932 chart, or a redrawing of it. Hevesy, a Hungarian living in Paris, noticed resemblances between Indus seal signs and rongorongo, concluded rongorongo came from the Indus, and the idea was initially well received and debated through the 1930s. So the mainstream isn't blind to this; it's one of the most examined comparisons in the history of undeciphered scripts, and it lost for three specific reasons that are still the whole argument:

Fidelity. Miano's point is the historically important one. These are drawings, not tablet photographs, and critics from Métraux onward noted that the rongorongo forms were redrawn rather than taken from the tablets, with the resemblances concentrated among the simplest signs. In several pairs the redrawing does the work.
Selection over a quarter-million candidates. With 400–600 signs on each side there are somewhere between 160,000 and 360,000 possible pairings. If one pair in ten thousand looks alike to a motivated eye, you get 16–36 matches. Twenty-four is what chance produces. Look at where they fall: stick-figure humans, an oval, an 8, a U, a lollipop. Those shapes have almost no design space; every pictographic system on earth has them. A real relationship shows up in the complex, arbitrary signs, and the chart has none.
Time and place. Indus writing stops around 1900 BCE. Rapa Nui was settled around 1200 CE by Polynesians, which linguistics, archaeology, and genetics agree on, and no intermediate script exists anywhere in between. Rongorongo's own date of creation is unknown, and none of its glyphs can actually be read.

On your eyes: this is the one situation where they're the wrong instrument. Human vision is superb at finding matches, and when the search space is a quarter-million pairs, "I can see it" is a statement about the size of the search, not about the data. The English-alphabet analogy fails for the same reason. We know Latin descends from Phoenician because the letters share order, sound values, a full-set correspondence, and a documented chain of transmission, not because 24 of them look alike. When Harappa.com put the question to specialists, Parpola said there is no historical connection, Vidale said no, and Vahia said the shared signs look like generic strokes and that one would need to set up standards of comparison such as stroke-count distributions and stylistic similarity before drawing conclusions. That last remark is your opening.

What would push the debate forward is the quantitative version of Hevesy that, as far as I can tell, nobody has published, and it's a weekend of work with AI:

Fidelity audit. For each of the 24 pairs, retrieve the real glyph: Barthel's rongorongo tracings and tablet photographs, and Mahadevan's or Wells's Indus sign list with seal photos from the Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions. Put chart drawing, real Indus, and real rongorongo side by side. Blind raters, human and vision-model, score real-to-real similarity. This alone tests your own condition, "if the image accurately represents what is there," and it's the cheapest thing you could do.
Null distribution. Assemble sign inventories for 20–30 unrelated systems: Cretan hieroglyphic, Linear A, Vinča, Luwian, proto-Elamite, oracle bone, Egyptian, Naxi Dongba, Mixtec, Mi'kmaq, Ojibwe, Nsibidi, Vai, Cherokee, Pahawh Hmong. Compute pairwise similarity (embeddings plus blind judgments at a fixed threshold) and count "nearly identical" matches for every script pair, then see where Indus–rongorongo ranks. If most unrelated pairs yield 20–40 matches, the chart is ordinary; if Indus–rongorongo is a clear outlier, that's a real finding. Pre-register the prediction that matches concentrate in low-complexity signs. There's recent precedent for this style of test: an April 2026 paper scored the Indus sign system against synthetic baselines and seven attested non-linguistic symbol systems, comparing distributional profiles rather than semantic content.
Structure. Related scripts share behavior, not just shapes: sign frequencies, positional preferences, pair constraints, ligature rules. Check whether the 24 matched signs behave alike in their corpora. If Indus sign X is a high-frequency terminal and rongorongo sign Y is a rare ligature component, the resemblance is skin-deep.

Publish it as a reproducible notebook with one chart of your own: the Indus–rongorongo match count plotted against the distribution for every other script pair.
