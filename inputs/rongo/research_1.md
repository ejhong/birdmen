# Do Easter Island and the Indus Valley Share a Script? A Fair Test of a Beautiful Coincidence

### The rongorongo–Indus resemblance is visually striking, almost a century old, and — I'll argue — almost certainly an artifact of how our eyes hunt for patterns. Here's the full story, and a pre-registered, AI-assisted plan to settle it.

**Epistemic status:** A synthesis of published scholarship plus a research proposal. The historical facts below are sourced and, where scholars disagree or a figure is uncertain, I say so. The verdict I lean toward — coincidence, not contact — is the current scholarly consensus, but the whole point of this piece is that the claim deserves a real test rather than an assertion, and I have not yet run that test.

---

On September 3, 2026, an old idea resurfaced in my feed. The account @ArchaicLens posted the famous chart — two columns of little line-drawings, Indus Valley seal signs on one side, Easter Island rongorongo glyphs on the other, about two dozen pairs that look uncannily alike. The argument: these pairs are "nearly identical," and waving them away as coincidence is "like finding the entire English alphabet in another language."

The historian David Miano (@DrDavidMiano) pushed back. His points, in plain prose: each script has somewhere between 400 and 600 distinct signs, so two dozen look-alikes is only about 4.8% of the inventory; none of the pairs is actually *identical*, only similar; and — crucially — the chart is not made of photographs of real glyphs but of hand drawings by someone who set out to highlight resemblances.

I find the resemblances genuinely striking. I understand why they grab people. And I think that is exactly why the question deserves a careful, adversarial, pre-registered test instead of another round of dueling assertions. This piece brings you fully up to speed on the claim's century-long history, on what we actually know about both scripts, and on the AI-assisted study my foundation (Athanatos) wants to run to settle it.

## What we actually know (the summary box)

- **The claim is old and specific.** In 1932 a Hungarian engineer, Guillaume de Hevesy, told the Paris Académie that dozens of Indus and rongorongo signs matched.
- **The two scripts are separated by roughly 20,000 km and thousands of years**, with no intermediate script attested anywhere in between.
- **Both scripts are undeciphered.** We cannot read either one, so any "match" is a match of *shape*, not of sound or meaning.
- **The Indus script** (c. 2600–1900 BCE) has ~400–700 signs depending on who counts, ~4,000+ short inscriptions averaging ~5 signs.
- **Rongorongo** survives on ~two dozen wooden objects, was likely created by Polynesians who settled Rapa Nui around 1150–1280 CE, and one tablet's wood dates to 1493–1509 CE.
- **Specialists overwhelmingly reject a historical connection**, mostly because the matches cluster among the simplest signs and because Hevesy's drawings were partly inaccurate.
- **No one has ever run a rigorous, quantitative, blinded comparison** of the two sign inventories against a proper null distribution. That is the gap this project fills.

## Part 1: The full background

### 1. Where the claim came from

Guillaume de Hevesy (also rendered Vilmos or Wilhelm de Hevesy) was a Hungarian living in Paris who dabbled in comparative linguistics. He noticed a resemblance between Indus Valley seal inscriptions and the rongorongo of Easter Island and drew the bold conclusion that rongorongo originated in the Indus civilization. He first published the idea in 1932, as a "Lettre de M. de Hevesy sur une écriture mystérieuse du bassin de l'Indus" in the *Comptes rendus* of the Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres (vol. 76, no. 3, p. 310). The comparison made an impression because it was presented to the Académie by the eminent French Orientalist Paul Pelliot, which lent it instant credibility, and the press picked it up. The journal *Nature* reported it in 1932, noting that Hevesy had "discovered that a number of the signs of the prehistoric Indian script on seals from Mohenjo-daro also appear in the script of the Easter Island inscribed wooden tablets."

How many parallels did Hevesy claim? The number grew. Popular accounts usually say he found about forty rongorongo signs with Indus counterparts. But by the time he published his fuller comparison plate in 1933 ("Sur une écriture océanienne paraissant d'origine néolithique," *Bulletin de la Société préhistorique française*), a contemporary summary (Billimoria 1939) reports that he claimed **130 signs** were similar, and one modern scholarly account (the pacific-credo study of the Franco-Belgian mission) describes "a table of nearly 150 sign-to-sign comparisons." Later writers often cite "between forty and fifty complex characters" as the strongest subset. So the honest statement is: Hevesy's *initial* claim was around forty parallels, later expanded into a table of well over a hundred.

His sources matter, because they turn out to be the weak point. For the Indus signs he leaned on the reproductions of Mohenjo-daro seals then newly available (John Marshall's 1931 volumes and G. R. Hunter's 1932 work). For rongorongo he did *not* work from the tablets themselves — there was no accessible corpus — but from secondary reproductions, including a photograph of a tablet in Katherine Routledge's 1919 *The Mystery of Easter Island* and other tracings.

The idea was well received at first, and it fed a diffusionist current in 1930s anthropology. Robert von Heine-Geldern, the Vienna prehistorian, corresponded with Hevesy and looked for related connections across Neolithic China and Southeast Asia. The enthusiasm ran far enough that in 1939 the *Journal of the Polynesian Society* published N. M. Billimoria's "The Panis of the Rig Veda and Script of Mohenjo Daro and Easter Island" (vol. 48, no. 2, pp. 92–103), which speculated that a wandering Vedic merchant people, the Panis, carried the writing from the Indus across Asia to the Pacific.

### 2. The critiques

The pushback was quick and, ultimately, decisive.

The central figure was the Swiss-Argentine-American ethnologist **Alfred Métraux**. His critical study "The Proto-Indian Script and the Easter Island Tablets. (A critical study)" appeared in *Anthropos* (vol. 33, 1938, pp. 218–239); Hevesy replied in the same volume, and Métraux restated his case in the monumental *Ethnology of Easter Island* (Bishop Museum Bulletin 160, 1940). Métraux argued that rongorongo had to be understood as an indigenous Polynesian creation in its Polynesian context, not dissociated from the island's own art and traditions, and he rejected the enormous leaps of time and space the Indus hypothesis required. Indeed, the 1934–35 Franco-Belgian expedition to Easter Island — led by Henri Lavachery and Métraux — was launched partly to test Hevesy's claim on the ground, and it came back skeptical. (A note on sourcing: I could confirm the substance of Métraux's arguments through multiple secondary channels, but the full text of his 1938 *Anthropos* article is paywalled, so I am not quoting it verbatim here.)

Three specific lines of criticism have hardened over the decades:

**(a) The drawings were unreliable.** Because there was no easy way to typeset or even reliably identify rongorongo glyphs at the time, several of the rongorongo forms in Hevesy's comparisons were, as Steven Roger Fischer later documented in his 1997 history *Rongorongo: The Easter Island Script*, simply *spurious* — as the Wikipedia summary of Fischer puts it, "it was not apparent that several of the rongorongo glyphs illustrated in Hevesy's publications were spurious." The criticism cuts both ways: pro-Indus writers concede that Hevesy's comparison fails "mostly on account of many faults in his transcription of the Indus signs." In other words, some of the matched pairs matched because they had been (consciously or not) drawn to match.

**(b) The matches cluster among the simplest signs.** The strongest "parallels" are stick figures, circles, U-shapes, and other minimal forms — exactly the shapes that recur in nearly every pictographic system on Earth because there are only so many ways to draw a person or a circle. On Harappa.com, the leading Indus scholars answer the question directly. Asko Parpola: "There is no historical connection, only imaginary." Massimo Vidale: "No." Mayank Vahia: "Many of the signs that are common seem to be generic strokes and I am not convinced they are greatly similar… One needs to set up standards of comparison using parameters such as the statistical distribution of number of strokes per sign, stylistic similarity etc." Nisha Yadav calls a connection "highly unlikely." Vahia's answer is, in effect, a call for exactly the kind of study I describe below.

**(c) Chronology and geography.** The Indus script flourished c. 2600–1900 BCE; rongorongo was carved by a people who reached Rapa Nui around 1200 CE. The gap is on the order of 4,000 years (some writers say 5,000) and roughly 19,000–20,000 km (some say 25,000), with no intermediate script attested anywhere along any plausible route. English Wikipedia's framing is representative: the two scripts are "separated by half the world and half of history (19,000 km (12,000 mi) and 4000 years), and had no known intermediate stages." Later assessments by Thomas Barthel (1958), Fischer (1997), Jacques Guy (whose kohaumotu.org / rongorongo.org remains the best corpus reference online), and Konstantin Pozdniakov have all treated the Indus link as a dead end.

**Where does the popular chart come from?** The paired-row chart circulating today descends directly from Hevesy's own comparison plate in his 1932–33 papers — a table of roughly 150 sign-to-sign comparisons that he built to make his case. It is *not* from Métraux (who was the critic, not the illustrator) and not originally from Wikipedia. In English-language popular culture the version most people have seen was reproduced and re-drawn through later books, notably Andrew Robinson's histories of writing (*The Story of Writing*, 1995; *Lost Languages*, 2002) — the boloji.com reproduction, for instance, explicitly captions its comparison image as "a sample from the History of Writing." The key point for readers: the chart is a *persuasion artifact* built by an advocate, not a neutral catalogue.

### 3. What is actually known about the Indus script

The Indus (Harappan) script belongs to the mature Indus Valley Civilization, c. 2600–1900 BCE. Estimates of the number of distinct signs differ sharply because scholars disagree about what counts as a separate sign versus a variant (allograph) or a ligature: Iravatham Mahadevan's 1977 concordance lists **417** signs (419 across 2,911 objects after late additions); Parpola's count is roughly **386–398**; Bryan Wells's 2015 inventory reaches **694–695**. The corpus is about **4,000–4,700 inscriptions** on seals, sealings, copper tablets, pottery, and ivory rods, and the texts are famously short — an average of about **five signs**, median four. Writing runs predominantly right-to-left, with boustrophedon in some cases. The script remains **undeciphered**.

Whether it even encodes language is itself contested. In 2009, Rajesh Rao and colleagues argued in *Science* ("Entropic Evidence for Linguistic Structure in the Indus Script," *Science* 324:1165) that the script's conditional entropy resembles that of natural languages. Richard Sproat, with Steve Farmer and Michael Witzel, countered that conditional entropy cannot distinguish linguistic from non-linguistic symbol systems, and that the Indus corpus looks more like a non-linguistic emblem system (texts too short, too many one-off signs, no long repeated formulae). This debate is directly relevant to my project because it produced the methodological tool I want to borrow: **synthetic and comparative baselines**. The most recent entry, an April 2026 arXiv paper by Ashish Nair, "How Non-Linguistic Is the Indus Sign System? A Synthetic-Baseline Scorecard" (arXiv:2604.17828), tests the Indus corpus against computer-generated "heraldic" and "administrative" non-linguistic systems on four metrics at once, and finds the Indus corpus sits in an intermediate zone that matches neither baseline cleanly (replicating a Zipf slope of −1.49 and a conditional entropy of 3.23 bits). Whatever you conclude, the lesson is the same: you learn what a pattern means only by comparing it to a properly constructed null.

There is also money and politics in the air: on January 5, 2025, at the International Seminar marking the centenary of the civilization's discovery, held in Chennai, Tamil Nadu Chief Minister M. K. Stalin announced a **US $1 million prize** — "I announce a cash prize of $1 million to individuals or organisations that decipher the script to the satisfaction of archaeological experts," he said — along with a Rs 2 crore endowed research chair named for Mahadevan. The key corpus resources are the *Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions* (CISI, Parpola et al.), Mahadevan's concordance, and Wells's ICIT.

### 4. What is actually known about rongorongo

Rongorongo survives on only about **two dozen wooden objects** (27 by the most inclusive current count). Thomas Barthel accepted 24 as genuine and labeled them A–Z; two more have been added since, so you'll see totals of 25 to 27 depending on the count. Most are oblong tablets, but the corpus also includes the Santiago Staff (a chieftain's baton, text I — the longest single text), two reimiro (pectoral ornaments, J and L), a tangata manu "birdman" statuette (X), and a snuff box assembled from a cut-up tablet (Y). One tablet, E (Keiti), was destroyed in a fire and survives only as a copy. Across the surviving artifacts, over 400 distinct glyphs have been identified among approximately **15,000 characters**, spread over about 13 well-preserved tablets.

Sign counts again depend on how you split. Barthel's 1958 catalogue *Grundlagen zur Entzifferung der Osterinselschrift* used "three-digit numbers to encode 800 possible glyphs (638 of which are used)," conventionally summarized as roughly **600** sign shapes. But Pozdniakov and Pozdniakov (2007) showed that most of those are variants and ligatures; on their analysis a basic inventory of **52 signs accounts for 99.7% of all the texts**. As one summary of their work notes, the three competing counts — 322, 120, and 52 — "differ considerably in size," and the choice matters enormously: a system with ~52 basic units behaves very differently, statistically, from one with 600.

The script is written in **reverse boustrophedon** (alternating direction line to line, with every other line upside-down). The only broadly accepted partial reading is a portion of the **Mamari tablet (C)**, which appears to encode a **lunar calendar** — though even that cannot actually be *read* in the linguistic sense. Steven Fischer's 1997 proposal that the Santiago Staff consists of "procreation triads" (X copulated with Y, there issued Z; his putative phallus sign occurs 564 times on the staff) drew wide attention but was sharply criticized by Guy and Pozdniakov and is not accepted. The field is split over whether rongorongo is **true writing** or **proto-writing / a mnemonic device**; since Butinov and Knorozov in the 1950s, the majority view leans toward the latter, which would make it undecipherable in principle.

Dating has swung recently. Europeans first documented the tablets in 1864, and one hypothesis held that rongorongo was invented only after the 1770 Spanish visit (which would make it a case of stimulus diffusion). But in February 2024, Silvia Ferrara and colleagues published radiocarbon dates for four tablets held in Rome (*Scientific Reports* 14:2794). Three (A Tahua, B Aruku Kurenga, C Mamari) dated to the 18th–19th centuries; the fourth, **Tablet D (Échancrée)**, yielded a calibrated wood-felling date "spanning between **1493 and 1509 cal AD** (68.3%)" — as Ferrara framed it, more than 200 years before the first recorded European arrival in the 1720s. Two caveats the authors were careful about: radiocarbon dates the *wood*, not the *inscription* (the "old wood" problem), and Tablet D is made of *Podocarpus* wood (cf. *latifolia*, per Orliac's identification), not native to Rapa Nui, which likely arrived as driftwood or ship salvage. Still, it strengthens the case that rongorongo was an independent Polynesian invention. Rapa Nui itself was settled around **1150–1280 CE** (broader estimates range from ~800 to 1200). The upshot: a **3,000-plus-year and ~20,000 km gap** from the Indus, with nothing in between.

### 5. Are there any modern quantitative comparisons of the two scripts?

As far as I can find, **no**. There is no published, rigorous statistical or computational test — sign-similarity metrics, stroke-count distributions, distributional comparisons — of the specific Indus–rongorongo relationship. The claim has lived for ninety years almost entirely on visual juxtaposition. That absence is precisely the opening for this project.

It's worth noting how *many* other origins have been proposed for rongorongo, because the ease of finding "parallels" is itself evidence. Erich von Hornbostel linked it to the Cuna (Cuña) pictographs of Panama and to Chinese "Yü Pei" signs; others have invoked Egyptian hieroglyphs, Shang-dynasty oracle-bone script, Sumatran ship-cloth patterns, South American systems, and even Easter Island's own string-figure (kai-kai) repertoire. When a single undeciphered script can be "connected" to that many unrelated systems, the resemblances are telling us about human pattern-matching, not about history.

### 6. The statistics you need to keep in your head

Here is why "trust your eyes" fails in this specific situation.

**The search space is huge.** If each script has 400–600 signs, the number of possible cross-script pairings is 400×400 to 600×600 — roughly **160,000 to 360,000 candidate pairs**. Suppose a motivated observer, free to rotate, reflect, and loosely match, judges just 1 in 10,000 pairs to "look alike." That alone predicts **16 to 36 apparent matches** by pure chance — right in the neighborhood of the two dozen on the chart. Finding ~24 look-alikes is therefore exactly what coincidence predicts; it is not a surplus that demands a historical explanation.

**The design space of simple signs is tiny.** A single vertical stroke, a circle, a U, a cross, a stick figure — these appear in the Indus script, in rongorongo, in Vinča marks, in Chinese, in tally systems, everywhere, because they are the cheapest shapes a hand can make. Matches among *these* carry almost no information. The signature of genuine relatedness is shared **arbitrary, complex** signs — elaborate forms that had no functional reason to be drawn that particular way, matching across both systems. That is the opposite of what the rongorongo–Indus chart shows, where (critics note) the matches concentrate among the simplest forms.

**Why the "English alphabet in another language" analogy fails.** We *know* the Latin alphabet descends from Greek and Phoenician not because of a couple of dozen look-alike letters, but because of four things the Indus–rongorongo case lacks entirely: a shared **sign order** (the abecedary sequence), shared **sound values** (aleph→alpha→A), **full-set correspondence** (essentially the whole inventory maps over, not 4.8% of it), and a **documented transmission chain** across contiguous cultures and centuries. Twenty-four look-alikes out of ~500, with no order, no sound values, no full-set mapping, and a 20,000-km gap, is not that. The analogy inverts the actual standard of evidence.

None of this *proves* there is no connection. It shows that the visual chart, however compelling, is consistent with pure coincidence — and that only a proper test can tell the difference.

## Part 2: The AI research plan

The goal is simple to state: **measure whether the Indus–rongorongo resemblance is greater than what we'd expect between two unrelated scripts** — and do it in a way that a skeptic and a believer agree on *in advance*.

### A. Fidelity audit — is the chart even real?

For each of the ~24 pairs on the popular chart, retrieve the *real* glyphs: the rongorongo forms from Barthel's tracings and from tablet photographs (the kohaumotu.org corpus, the Ferrara et al. 3D models), and the Indus forms from Mahadevan's or Wells's sign lists with seal photographs from CISI. Then place three images side by side for each pair: the chart drawing, the real Indus sign, and the real rongorongo glyph. **Blind raters — humans plus vision-language models — score real-to-real similarity without ever seeing the advocacy chart.** This directly tests Miano's charge that the chart is idealized hand-drawing. It answers the question: *if we only look at what is actually on the tablets and seals, how similar are these signs really?*

### B. Null distribution — how surprising is the match count?

Assemble sign inventories for 20–30 uncontroversially unrelated sign systems: Cretan hieroglyphic, Linear A, Vinča signs, Anatolian (Luwian) hieroglyphs, proto-Elamite, Chinese oracle-bone script, Egyptian hieroglyphs, Naxi Dongba, Mixtec and Aztec pictography, Mi'kmaq hieroglyphs, Ojibwe birchbark pictographs, Nsibidi, Vai, Cherokee, Pahawh Hmong, and more. For **every pair** of scripts, compute pairwise visual similarity (image embeddings, plus blind human/VLM judgments at a fixed pre-set threshold) and count how many "nearly identical" matches each pair produces. Then see **where Indus–rongorongo ranks** in that distribution. If it's unremarkable — if Egyptian–Cherokee or Vinča–Dongba throw up just as many look-alikes — the claim collapses into noise. We pre-register the prediction that matches will concentrate among **low-complexity signs**, and that a true relatedness signal, if it existed, would show up among **high-complexity, arbitrary signs**. This borrows the synthetic-baseline logic of the April 2026 Indus scorecard paper and Sproat's comparison corpora.

### C. Structural comparison — do the signs *behave* alike?

Related scripts don't just share shapes; they share **behavior**. For the specific 24 "matched" signs, test whether each behaves the same way in its own corpus: sign **frequency**, **positional** preference (initial vs. final), **pairing** constraints, **ligature** rules, and **stroke-count distributions** (exactly the parameter Mayank Vahia said was the missing standard of comparison). If Indus sign X and rongorongo sign X' are genuinely the "same" sign, they should occupy similar structural niches. If they don't, the shape-match is cosmetic.

### D. The credibility layer

This is where Athanatos's principles live:

- **Pre-register** the hypotheses, coding scheme, sample, statistics, and explicit win/lose criteria on OSF *before* running anything.
- Keep coders **blind** to which script a sign comes from and to the study's hypothesis.
- Publish **code, data, and results as a reproducible notebook regardless of outcome** — including if the result surprises me and favors the connection.
- Invite an **adversarial reviewer from the skeptical side** (and, ideally, one sympathetic to the claim) to sign off on the protocol before data collection.
- The **headline deliverable** is a single chart: the Indus–rongorongo match count plotted against the full distribution of match counts for all other script pairs, with its percentile marked.

If Indus–rongorongo lands in the fat middle of that distribution, the honest conclusion is "coincidence, as expected." If it lands far out in the tail — especially driven by complex, arbitrary signs that also behave alike structurally — then I will have found something genuinely worth arguing about, and I'll say so loudly.

### E. This is case study #1 of a "Deep Memory" test bench

The rongorongo–Indus question is the first trial of a broader instrument I'm building to test claims of **long-distance motif transmission** with the same discipline. The next candidates include the recurring **bird–serpent–"handbag"** iconographic complex that turns up across Jiroft, Assyrian reliefs, Olmec carving, Göbekli Tepe, and — yes — Rapa Nui. The method is identical every time: **provenanced corpora, blind structured coding, calibration on cases where the answer is already known** (related scripts that we *know* are related, and unrelated ones we *know* are not), and **pre-registered predictions**. The aim is not to debunk or to vindicate, but to build a reusable machine that tells us how surprised we should be by any given resemblance.
