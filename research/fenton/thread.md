### A2 · buckets

> The famous bags/buckets are another key element of pillar 43 at Gobekli Tepe, a site famously
> covered in serpent art. One example is given below right.
>
> Buckets and serpents portrayed on large rocks are a tradition that goes back much further than
> 12,000 years ago.
>
> The three animals above the buckets on pillar 43 are also important, being clan totems—more on
> that later. Below left is an image of an ancient bark bucket used in prehistoric times in
> Australia. The text below is a snippet of prehistoric lore, making the links clearer. We will
> tackle the importance of the sacred yam later.

Image: `bush-bucket.jpg` (IMG_8506). The image contains a drawing of a painted bark bucket, a
detail of one handled shape on Pillar 43, and this printed excerpt, which the post does not
attribute:

> Bush buckets, as attributes of primeval heroes and portrayed in rock pictures, appear as local
> totems of the clan territories (támbun) of Ayángara on the Lower King Edward River and Yálamba,
> that is said to be situated somewhere on the Prince Regent River. Just as in Ayángara, there too
> the pictorial representation of a karáki is to be seen under a ledge. We were told abut this,
> that Wúrulawúrula, the mythical ancestor of the sprouting big yam (kanmángu) and local Wóndjina
> [74] of Yálamba, had gone about in the bush and dug up yams. He hung his bush bucket filled with
> many tubers up on a massive stone and then he lay down to rest. At this spot Wúrulawúrula also
> went into the ground and became an Ungud serpent. The pictures of himself and the filled bush
> bucket then remained behind on the rock in the usual form.

[The rivers, the Wóndjina and Ungud terms and the orthography match Andreas Lommel's ethnography
of the Unambal (Wunambal) of the north-west Kimberley, first published in German in 1952 and in
English in 1997. The attribution is this project's inference, not the post's.]

### A5 · Yinganna

> We must introduce a key figure, Yinganna, a creation spirit and mother goddess figure. She
> carried her bark buckets across the landscape; inside each was a tribe of people and their
> associated animal totems and lore.
>
> Yinganna is an alter-ego of the infamous rainbow serpent, generally represented as a snake but
> sometimes portrayed as a chimaera. Notice her strange head, which is shaped like a sacred yam
> leaf.

Image: `yinganna.jpg` (IMG_8513).

### A6 · the female figure

> It is well established that Gobekli Tepe has a glaring absence of female art. However, one image
> of a presumed goddess is known from the 'Lion Building'.
>
> This engraving has two essential features. The first is the position, which indicates childbirth
> (the female genitalia are prominent and cartoonish). The second feature is the head, which
> appears almost like a mushroom. However, in return to Northern Australia and Yinganna, here we
> see her represented in the same birthing position, including exaggerated genitalia.
>
> What looks like a mushroom is none other than a sacred yam leaf, often depicted instead of her
> head. We can see more of these yam leaves erupting from the back of a rainbow serpent painted in
> its chimaera form (note the two feathers on the head; it is a feathered serpent).

Image: `birthing-figure.jpg` (IMG_8515).

### A7 · snakes

> Gobekli tepe is covered in snakes. There is no getting away from them. Clearly, snakes played a

> That's because this is no ordinary snake; it is a rainbow serpent, and the head is again akin to
> what we have seen in art representing the serpentine mother goddess Yinganna. Note here that
> rainbow serpents are dual, with male and female forms.

### B2 · the drowned heartland

> Over thousands of years, this advanced maritime culture of Island Southeast Asia expanded its
> reach and sphere of influence to encompass the northern coast of Australia and the southern
> coasts of Asia, even carrying cultural elements as far afield as Africa and the Americas. Then, a
> series of catastrophes (likely a combination of comet impact and solar outbursts) initiated a
> profound climatic shift and a 130-metre sea level rise.
>
> While this did not destroy their world overnight, the coastal settlements that constitute
> maritime civilizations sank beneath the ocean one by one. The idyllic equatorial heartlands of
> their world, the Sunda plate, almost entirely vanished.

map with the land exposed at lowered sea level coloured red.

### B3 · many human species

> We must remember that the Sunda, Sahul, and the Islands of Southeast Asia 40,000 years ago were
> like Middle Earth. The region was not limited to modern humans; instead, many human species were
> potentially linked by boat journeys.
>
> We know there were Denisovans, possibly multiple sub-species, Neanderthals, Homo erectus, Homo
> floresiensis and maybe still Homo luzonensis, plus ghost populations inferred by DNA studies.
> Siling would have almost certainly brought different populations into contact, potentially
> explaining much of the unexpected genomic data from the region, including Papuan modern human
> genomes having up to 6% Denisovan DNA from at least three divergent source populations!

Papuans", Cell 177(4). Not republished; the paper is cited instead.

> How could people from Oceania reach Brazil tens of thousands of years ago and not even pass

> In a ship, that's how—possibly bringing with them either Denisovan and Neanderthal crewmates or,
> more likely, being themselves hybrids, as suggested by the evidence of DNA segments unique to

> There are clear indications of a northward migration of these prehistoric populations.
